Title: Evaluation system needs revolution
Term: Optimization - Spring 2026
Posted: 5/9/2026 6:09 AM GMT-7
Rating: 3.8
Difficulty: 5.2
Textbook: 1
Lectures: 3
Professor: 4
Piazza: 5
Workload: 16 hours per week

Review:
Pros:
1. Some insights into the underlying optimization schemes
2. TAs are responsive
3. Avoid in-depth math concepts and make it a little bit easier for comprehension

Cons:
1. The evaluation system is a disaster
2. No coding
3. Not well-designed materials
4. Lack of cutting-edge contents

Detailed Review:
This is my fourth course. In fact, I registered for OLO at first. But I found OLO's lectures hard to follow on the first day of class and seemed to require a foundation in optimization, so I dropped OLO and registered for OPT.

The topics included in this course are good. This course mainly introduces some basic concepts in convex optimization, linear programs (LP), duality, KKT conditions, and many kinds of gradient descent methods. All these concepts play significant roles in optimization. However, the pace and the evaluation system are not satisfactory.

This course spends approximately half of the semester introducing LP and duality. I don't think LP is a technique that requires so much time to comprehend. This leads to a very fast pace in introducing gradient descent in the remaining part of the semester. As a result, I have to learn simple gradient descent, subgradient method, line search, projected gradient descent, Frank-Wolfe, proximal gradient descent, Newton method, Quasi-Newton method, and SGD in 6 weeks. I think the course should be adjusted, and reduce the time in introducing LP and duality, and enable us to have more time to work on the gradient descent part.

The lectures are recorded by two professors. Prof Sanghavi has a very different accent, so I sometimes found it a little bit confusing to understand what he says. Prof Caramanis's lectures are good, but I think there is still room for improvement in image resolution.

The materials are not satisfactory either. The date on the provided lecture notes made by Prof Sanghavi clearly shows that all materials were made in 2019, which is almost 7 years ago. If you think that's not a big issue, the errors in the lecture notes will surely confuse you. I have to refer to the lecture notes published by CMU when preparing for exams. I don't know why he preferred to offer a typed version instead of the handwritten version in the lectures, as there are fewer errors in the handwritten ones. Prof Caramanis's lecture notes are handwritten, but sometimes the font size is too small, and the stroke width is too big, making the lecture notes illegible.

The evaluation system of this course includes homework and exams. This system is a total disaster. Let me explain this in detail:

There are 9 theoretical assignments. The lowest one is dropped in common (we can drop 2 in this semester due to technical issues with Feedback Fruits). However, homework only counts for 30% of the total grade, which is the lowest proportion among the courses I have taken. There are so many assignments. Sometimes there are a LOT of sub-questions in one question. I can't forget homework 2, where the last question includes 11 sub-questions (requires answering 5 of them). My solution includes 15+ pages for each assignment, commonly 20+ pages. If I compile all my solutions into a book, the book may be thicker than my master's dissertation (I'm pursuing a second master's degree now; I wrote a dissertation during my first master's program).

There are also questions asking for algorithm implementation. I don't understand why this course does not include any coding homework while asking us to implement what we have learned in class in theoretical homework, and requires us to share the code in the submission. They could have split the homework into theoretical and coding parts, but they just left all implementations in the theoretical homework.

The provided solutions sometimes include errors as well. I believe we are not the first to identify those errors. However, the professors choose not to correct them and reuse these materials semester after semester. Besides, remember the implementation I mentioned above? The solutions to these questions are often two words -- happy implementing... I'm not happy. As a solution, you should at least give us exemplary code and figures for implementation, and these are vital to peer grading!

Peer grading is not well-designed as well. You get full marks, 50%, or 0 on one question, no fine-grained score. Mostly, your peers will give you 100% because the grading guidance tells you that partial errors are acceptable as long as the thoughts are correct. However, I still encountered some students not giving me the full marks even though I was totally correct. They may, in fact, not understand the concepts so well, and gave me low grades based on their comprehension. In my opinion, peer grading is not an ideal choice for evaluating the homework, because not every student is a qualified grader if he/she fails to digest the contents in class. Fortunately, the head TA could regrade. I highly appreciate the responsive TAs.

The exam is the most disastrous part of the evaluation system. There are 2 midterms + 1 final, and all exams only include a few multiple-choice questions (midterm 1 has 15 questions, midterm 2 has 12 questions, final has 20 questions). That means that 70% of your grade relies on 47 MC questions. This is too crazy for a theoretical course. If you do wrong on one question, you lose all the marks for that question, and your final grade decreases for 1% or more. Most problems in the exams are calculation. I believe it's a good choice to use an exam format like ML, which allows you to show the process for getting the answer.

It's also a bad arrangement to have 2 midterms. There is only a short interval between midterm 2 and the final, which means that 50% of your grade is determined by your performance in the last several weeks. More importantly, the majority of the remaining materials (Newton method, Quasi-Newton, SGD) are not included in the final, which makes the scope of the final approximately the scope of midterm1 + midterm2, making the final redundant. I would treat the final exam as a measure for the professors to handle the remaining 30% of the total grade. If I had a chance to design the evaluation system, I would cancel the final and supplement some coding assignments, and make those homework counts for 30% of the total grade (in fact, they had an evaluation system like this in OLO, where exams only count for 30% of the total grade).

The only advantage is that the exams are not proctored. However, you still need to prepare for some calculations, which could make the midterms a little bit intensive. The statistics show that a quarter of this cohort gets full marks. This may urge the professors to turn to proctored exams in the future. OLO has turned to proctored exams, and I believe the tomorrow of OPT is today of OLO. Considering the large proportion of exam grades in the total grade, it is doomed to be hard to get a good letter grade in the future, at least harder than OLO.

To sum up, I think the topics are good for this course, but the evaluation system requires a revolution. If you are not a math genius and want to earn an A, this course may not be a good choice for you in the future.

Title: Good course if you like theory

Pros:

2. Really helps with understanding how ML actually works under the hood
3. Homework assignments really helped to solidify concepts

Cons:
1. Prof. Sanghavi's lectures were dry and sometimes hard to follow
2. Some topics covered once in lecture and never seen again
3. Pacing was a little off - algorithms felt rushed, LPs dragged on

Detailed Review:
I thought this was a solidly above average course, but it isn't for everyone. This is a math-heavy theory course. If you would rather focus on applications, this probably isn't for you. I think the first half of ML is a good comparison - if you found that interesting, you'll probably like this. If you didn't like ML, you'll hate Optimization.

It helps to have a good grasp on linear algebra (particularly eigenvalues and eigenvectors) going into the class. There is some opportunity to pick it up if you don't, but a lot of the math revolves around that, especially after the first exam.

Lectures are ok. This is a math class, after all, it's hard to make them super interesting. The general rhythm is Prof. Sanghavi giving a formal overview with proofs and then Prof. Constantine diving deeper into key concepts with worked examples. Prof. Sanghavi's lectures are decent if a little dry, and he moves pretty quickly sometimes. There are also a lot of errors in his typed slides (the downloadable PDFs - the handwritten slides in the videos are mostly fine).

Prof. Constantine doesn't cover as much breadth, but he spends a lot of time explaining concepts in detail. His lectures were the ones I re-watched to do homework problems and study for exams. My only complaint is that Prof. Constantine disappears in the last couple of weeks - I honestly had a less solid grasp on the material without him.

I thought the textbook was useful, but it's not an easy read. The value is that it gives slightly different explanations and examples for a lot of topics, and there were definitely a couple of exam questions I got right because I had read the relevant section of the book. You could get through the course without it, and I definitely didn't read the whole thing, but it helped in several cases when I didn't quite get an idea presented in lecture. And it's free online anyway.

The exams in this class are pretty challenging and make up 70% of the grade combined, but there is a significant curve at the end of the semester. A lot of the questions are fairly tricky and will test your ability to think through multiple details of a problem. Homeworks are a mix of straightforward problems and some slightly tricky, nuanced applications. The good news is you'll get full credit as long as you make a reasonable attempt at a problem, even if you don't get it right. Peer grading is a little annoying, but not any worse than ML. Plus almost everyone gets the full 5% for grading quality, so it's a nice boost to your grade at least.

Detailed Review:
The prior reviews for this course continue to be relevant. Sanghavi's lectures, which serve as the primary way of teaching the information in this class, seem to not be updated from 2018/2019. The content of this class isn't cutting edge so this is alright, but the number of unaddressed typos and errors in the lectures only made them harder to get through. The same goes for assignments and their keys, which is unacceptable because this class clearly recycles its material semester after semester.

The textbook is mostly beyond the scope of the class, and gaining useful information out of it is mostly not worth your time, especially after the first few introductory weeks. Sometimes the homework problems relate to an example from Sanghavi or Caramanis' lectures, but otherwise there tends to be a gap between the lecture and homework material necessitating outside studying or going to office hours for help (which was very helpful with this semester's TA, hopefully it stays that way in the future).

The material in this class is split mostly halfway between convex optimization/duality in the first half and optimization algorithms in the second half. The convex optimization section was taught decently well, with a heavy focus on duality and examples of it being included. The optimization algorithms in the second half are somewhat rushed, with a bunch of variants of gradient descent being introduced in the last 3 weeks with very basic coverage and little to no coverage at all on the homeworks which was disappointing.

The exams are all multiple choice and tend to be unrelated to the lectures and homeworks, not by the material necessarily but by the types of questions that are asked. There is a much heavier emphasis on reasoning through theorems and doing short numerical examples, which makes sense given the 1 hour/2 hour time periods for midterms and final exam respectively, but this disconnect makes it hard to prepare adequately for the exam. The best you can do, especially for the first exam, is to have a strong understanding of the concepts and be able to reason about them quickly. Regardless, the heavy curve applied at the end of this semester makes this class more bearable at least.

Although this is not particularly a bad thing, don't come into it expecting a lot of practical problems to work on. Several of the homework problems involved proving certain theorems, performing computations from lecture, and implementing some of the algorithms in python. The material itself was interesting, for example seeing how the dual formulation could be used prove seemingly unrelated theorems.

Lectures: There are two lecturers. The first one goes over the base of the course and has overall just decent lectures. The good thing is that the slides were attached to the edx webpage. Exams: The exams were pretty difficult, with the class averages being very low on all of them. They are all multiple choice, so there is no partial credit. This isn't ideal for a course like this were you can get a problem 95% right but still get no credit due to the all or nothing format. On the first exam, there were several "select all that apply" with over 6 possible choices. Several students complained and they largely reduced these for the two subsequent exams, which made those exams much more manageable. The time limit, particularly the one hour limit for the midterms, also seemed too short for the amount of questions asked.

Textbook: The official textbook was Convex Optimization by Boyd. This is a pretty famous textbook for being the go to book on optimization. It is also known to be a very difficult read with the book riddled with tons of complex math notation. The text could be useful in some sections of the course, but it does require a significant amount of time to parse the information in it.

Overall I enjoyed the subject and have a better appreciation for the subject matter. I also came out stronger at math than I was going in, so that's always a positive.
